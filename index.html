Course Overview

This course provides an overview of the historical context for the emergence of international justice mechanisms since Nuremberg and Tokyo, and explores the main mechanisms to fight impunity for international crimes at the international and domestic levels. It covers general principles of criminal law, complementarity, extraterritorial jurisdiction, gender-based violence, and victim participation and reparation.

The course adopts a practitioner's perspective, using specific cases to illustrate current challenges facing international and internationalised tribunals, with a focus on the International Criminal Court and on domestic courts exercising jurisdiction over international crimes. Throughout, we examine emerging themes: new technologies and open source evidence, corporate accountability, universal jurisdiction, and the strategic use of the International Court of Justice by states.

Part I: Oral advocacy (30%)

Moot oral statement before the 2026 ICC Assembly of States Parties, delivered in Session 6, Tuesday 13 October 2026.
  • Each of you will represent a State (party or non-party to the Rome Statute), an intergovernmental organisation (IGO) or a non-governmental organisation (NGO) on a specific issue or challenge during the General Debate of the 2026 Assembly of States Parties. The Assembly's website carries the official documents and agenda: asp.icc-cpi.int
  • You may invent an NGO, as long as it is clearly identified
  • For inspiration, look at the General Debate statements from previous years
  • The statement is strictly limited to three minutes and is delivered in front of your fellow representatives. Three minutes is usually 300 to 500 words
  • You may read from a written statement or speak without notes. You do not need to send me the written version

Grading indicators

  • Presentation: argumentation and analysis, adapted to the audience
  • Structure: coherence and use of time
  • Performance: contact with the audience
  • Illustration: making the issue concrete and understandable

Deadline

  • Sunday 11 October 2026: email your choice of State, IGO or NGO, and the name you will act under (for example an ambassador before the United Nations)

Part II: Written contribution on an emerging ICL issue (50%)

  • A legal contribution resulting from your research on an emerging issue in international criminal law, to be submitted by Tuesday 8 December 2026, 23:59 Paris time, as a PDF by email to jeanne.sulzer@sciencespo.fr
  • The contribution should have a legal focus, with attention to current academic and advocacy debates, the state of the jurisprudence (if any), legal characterisation and procedural issues

Examples of issues currently debated in the field, with developments before domestic and international courts and involvement from civil society:

  • Immunities
  • International criminal law and the migration crisis
  • Corporate accountability for international crimes
  • Climate change, ecocide and international criminal law
  • Open source investigation (OSINT) and new technologies to document and prove international crimes
  • International criminal law and cultural heritage
  • Gender apartheid
  • State litigation at the ICJ and the enforcement of provisional measures

You may choose another topic, to be agreed in advance by email.

Format

  • Times New Roman 12, single spaced
  • Internal citations, with references listed alphabetically in MLA format
  • Length: 4,000 words, excluding references
  • No more than two text boxes; illustrations and pictures are welcome and do not count towards the word limit

What a strong contribution looks like

  • A clear, arguable thesis rather than a description
  • Evidence from primary sources (cases, statutes, official documents) and engagement with scholarly sources
  • Analysis rather than summary; every claim cited; a conclusion that returns to the thesis

Part III: Class participation and discussion (20%)

Class participation is central, and students are expected to contribute actively to the discussion. Be prepared to bring up a point, raise a question, connect ideas, offer your opinion, share your experience, agree or disagree with others. Listening carefully to others in class should prompt ideas of your own. Express them, and do so respectfully.

Use of artificial intelligence

This course follows Sciences Po's policy on artificial intelligence: AI as a lever for your skills, never a substitute for your own judgement, with documented and acknowledged use. I use these tools myself to prepare materials and slides, and I check every output against the primary sources.

You may use generative artificial intelligence for the written contribution on one condition: attach a short appendix reproducing the prompts you used and stating how you verified the output. The appendix does not count towards the word limit. Undeclared use is a breach of academic integrity; declared use earns transparency, not marks. The analysis remains your responsibility, and superficial work will be graded as such. For the moot, the statement you deliver must be your own.

No assessment will require a paid tool, and you must not submit personal, sensitive or confidential data to public artificial intelligence platforms.
