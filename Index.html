Sciences Po Paris School of Law. Master's course, Fall 2026

Instructor: Jeanne Sulzer

Course Overview

This course provides an overview of the historical context for the emergence of international justice mechanisms since Nuremberg and Tokyo, and explores the main mechanisms to fight impunity for international crimes at the international and domestic levels. It covers general principles of criminal law, complementarity, extraterritorial jurisdiction, gender-based violence, and victim participation and reparation.

The course adopts a practitioner's perspective, using specific cases to illustrate current challenges facing international and internationalised tribunals, with a focus on the International Criminal Court and on domestic courts exercising jurisdiction over international crimes. Throughout, we examine emerging themes: new technologies and open source evidence, corporate accountability, universal jurisdiction, and the strategic use of the International Court of Justice by states.

Session 1: Origins of International Justiceopen / close
Tuesday 8 September 2026, 08:00 to 10:00. Salle C.S25, 1 place Saint-Thomas d'Aquin
The birth of modern international criminal justice at Nuremberg and Tokyo: individual criminal responsibility, the core crimes, the victor's justice critique, and the line from 1945 to the ICC. The session also introduces the hybrid character of international criminal procedure, which draws on both the common law and the civil law traditions.
Compulsory
Reading
An Introduction to International Criminal Law and Procedure (Cryer and others, 5th ed., 2024), section 2.2 (from page 29).Course manual, online through the Sciences Po library.
Listening
JusticeInfo, Nuremberg: judges were no longer seated in the centre (Christian Delage, 22 November 2025): listen to the linked RFI panel podcast featuring William Schabas.Open access.
Watching
The Nuremberg Trials Explained (Memorium Nuremberg Trials, Explainity).Short animated explainer.
Optional
  • Mark Kersten, Piercing the Colonial Veil? The War Nexus and the Limits of Nuremberg's Crimes Against Humanity, JICJ 23(2) (2025) 329.DOI, and Sciences Po library.
  • ICC, statement by the judges on the 80th anniversary of the Nuremberg trial (June 2025).Primary source.
Discussion questions
Foundations
  • Why did accountability wait until 1945? What made Nuremberg possible then, and not after 1918?
  • What was legally new: individual responsibility, crimes against peace, crimes against humanity? Which was most contested?
Critique and today
  • Was Nuremberg victor's justice, a genuine advance, or both? Does nullum crimen sine lege weaken it?
  • Is a Nuremberg for Ukraine, Gaza, or the DRC possible or desirable? What would it require, and what would it risk?
Session 2: The Crimes and Their Definitionsopen / close
Tuesday 15 September 2026, 08:00 to 10:00. Salle C.S25, 1 place Saint-Thomas d'Aquin
What counts as an international crime, and who decides. We work through the definitions of the four core crimes (genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, aggression), the deliberate exclusion of political groups from the Genocide Convention, and the moving frontier of the field: ecocide, gender apartheid, and other crimes still to be recognised.

An Introduction to International Criminal Law and Procedure (Cryer and others, 5th ed., 2024), chapters on genocide, crimes against humanity and war crimes.Course manual, online through the Sciences Po library.
Listening
Asymmetrical Haircuts, Episode 141: Defining Genocide with Melanie O'Brien (September 2025).Open access.
Watching
Rithy Panh, S-21: The Khmer Rouge Killing Machine (documentary, 2003), excerpt.On atrocities at the edge of the Convention's definition. Sciences Po library.
Optional
  • Beth Van Schaack, The Crime of Political Genocide: Repairing the Genocide Convention's Blind Spot, 106 Yale Law Journal 2259 (1997).The classic on the political groups gap. Open access.
  • William Schabas, Genocide in International Law: The Crime of Crimes (2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2009), chapter on protected groups.Sciences Po library.
  • Stop Ecocide Foundation, Independent Expert Panel: Legal Definition of Ecocide (June 2021).Primary source, co-chaired by Philippe Sands.

Discussion questions
The core crimes and the gap
  • Why did the drafters exclude political and social groups from the Genocide Convention? Whose interests did that protect?
  • If political targeting falls outside genocide, does the label matter for justice, or for the victims?
The frontier
  • Should ecocide sit alongside genocide and war crimes? Should apartheid be extended from race to gender?
  • Who should decide what becomes an international crime: states, courts, or civil society campaigns?
Session 3: General Principles of ICL and Obstacles to Accountabilityopen / close
Tuesday 22 September 2026, 08:00 to 10:00. Salle C.S25, 1 place Saint-Thomas d'Aquin
The general part of international criminal law: individual criminal responsibility, the modes of liability (Article 25), superior or command responsibility (Article 28), the mental element (Article 30) and defences. How guilt is attributed, and to whom, when the crimes are collective. The second half of the session turns to the obstacles: immunities of heads of state and officials, amnesties, statutory limitations and the tension between the ICJ Arrest Warrant judgment and Article 27 of the Rome Statute. Cases: Tadic, Pinochet, Arrest Warrant (DRC v. Belgium).
Compulsory
Reading
An Introduction to International Criminal Law and Procedure (Cryer and others, 5th ed., 2024), chapters on individual criminal responsibility and modes of liability.Course manual, online through the Sciences Po library.
Listening
Asymmetrical Haircuts, Episode 15: All the way to the top with Elies van Sliedregt.Modes of liability and command responsibility. Open access.
Watching
Not applicable for this session.
Optional
  • Rome Statute of the ICC, Articles 25, 27, 28 and 30 to 33.Primary source.
  • Damien Scalia, Genocidaire(s): au coeur de la justice internationale penale (Dalloz, 2021).Responsibility from the perpetrator's side. Sciences Po library.
  • Philippe Sands, Torture Team: Deception, Cruelty and the Compromise of Law (2008).Responsibility of lawyers and officials. Sciences Po library.
  • ICJ, Arrest Warrant of 11 April 2000 (Democratic Republic of the Congo v. Belgium), Judgment of 14 February 2002: head of state immunity.Primary source.
  • House of Lords, R v. Bow Street Metropolitan Stipendiary Magistrate, ex parte Pinochet Ugarte (No. 3), 24 March 1999.Primary source.
Discussion questions
Attributing guilt
  • International crimes are collective, yet criminal law is individual. How does ICL bridge that gap through the modes of liability?
  • Article 28 holds commanders liable for failing to prevent or punish. Is that fault, or close to strict liability? Is it fair?
Defences
  • Should superior orders ever excuse? Where does the Rome Statute draw the line, and why?
Obstacles
  • Can a sitting head of state be prosecuted? Reconcile the Arrest Warrant judgment with Article 27 of the Rome Statute and the practice on Al-Bashir and Putin.
  • Are amnesties ever compatible with the duty to prosecute international crimes?
Session 4: ICC Selective Prosecutionopen / close
Tuesday 29 September 2026, 08:00 to 10:00. Salle C.S25, 1 place Saint-Thomas d'Aquin
Twenty years of ICC practice seen through its critics. Why has the Court concentrated on some situations, many of them in Africa, and not others? We examine prosecutorial discretion, the Policy on Case Selection, complementarity, and the structural constraints (referrals, cooperation, the Security Council) that shape where the Court can act.
Compulsory

An Introduction to International Criminal Law and Procedure (Cryer and others, 5th ed., 2024), chapters on the ICC, jurisdiction, admissibility and complementarity.Course manual, online through the Sciences Po library.
Listening
Asymmetrical Haircuts, Justice Update: Are Preliminary Examinations at ICC working? With Patryk Labuda, on the Prosecutor's decision not to proceed on Iraq/UK.Open access.

International Criminal Court, ICC at a Glance (institutional video).ICC official channel.
Optional

  • Philippe Sands, Lawless World: America and the Making and Breaking of Global Rules (2005).Sciences Po library.
Discussion questions
Selectivity
  • Is the ICC's focus on certain situations evidence of bias, or a result of its jurisdictional design?
  • Has the opening of situations outside Africa (Ukraine, Palestine, Venezuela, the Philippines) answered the selectivity critique, or shifted it?
Complementarity
  • Complementarity makes the ICC a court of last resort. When should it defer to a national process, and when is a national process a shield?

Session 8: Universal Jurisdiction and Corporate Accountabilityopen / close
Tuesday 10 November 2026, 08:00 to 10:00. Salle C.S25, 1 place Saint-Thomas d'Aquin
When international courts cannot or will not act, national courts step in under universal jurisdiction, and increasingly against companies. We map universal jurisdiction as permanent state infrastructure (Germany, France, and the Syria cases), and follow the Lafarge case: the first time a corporation has faced a charge of complicity in crimes against humanity. First session after the Toussaint break.

Universal jurisdiction
  • Germany's Koblenz judgment and France's Lafarge case suggest universal jurisdiction is now operational. What changed?

Corporate accountability
  • Lafarge is the first corporation charged with complicity in crimes against humanity. Should criminal law reach companies, or is that a category error?

Session 9: New Technologies and International Criminal Lawopen / close
Tuesday 17 November 2026, 08:00 to 10:00. Salle C.S25, 1 place Saint-Thomas d'Aquin
How technology is reshaping the field, for better and for worse. On one side, open source investigation and digital evidence (satellite imagery, geolocation, social media) are transforming how atrocities are documented, under standards such as the Berkeley Protocol. On the other, the same tools bring new dangers: cyber operations as possible international crimes, deepfakes and fabricated evidence, and the risks of artificial intelligence in investigation and judgment.
Compulsory
Reading
Berkeley Protocol on Digital Open Source Investigations (OHCHR and the Human Rights Center, Berkeley).Primary source.
Listening
Asymmetrical Haircuts, Episode 161: Seeing Isn't Believing: Deepfakes and Evidence with Yvonne McDermott Rees and Anne Hausknecht (June 2026).Open access.
Watching
Forensic Architecture, Saydnaya: Inside a Syrian Torture Prison (video reconstruction).Open access.
Optional
  • An Introduction to International Criminal Law and Procedure (Cryer and others, 5th ed., 2024), on evidence.Course manual, online through the Sciences Po library.
  • Bellingcat: open source investigations, including work on deepfakes and verification.The positive and the negative of open source.
  • Human Rights Center, Berkeley: the Berkeley Protocol and its application.
Discussion questions
The promise
  • Open source evidence democratises documentation but floods courts with material of uncertain provenance. How does the Berkeley Protocol respond?
The risks
  • Artificial intelligence can sort millions of images, but can also fabricate them. Is AI a tool for accountability, a threat to it, or both?
  • Can a cyber operation be a war crime or a crime against humanity under the Rome Statute? What would that require?
Session 10: Strategic Litigation at the ICJ: Justice, Politics, Poweropen / close
Tuesday 24 November 2026, 08:00 to 10:00. Salle C.S25, 1 place Saint-Thomas d'Aquin
States before the World Court. Since 2019, third states have brought genocide claims to the ICJ on behalf of victims who cannot litigate themselves: The Gambia against Myanmar, South Africa against Israel, Ukraine against Russia. These cases are at once genuine accountability efforts and instruments of diplomatic strategy. The session examines what state litigation at the ICJ can deliver that individual prosecution at the ICC cannot, and the reverse; who has access to the Court and who does not; how provisional measures are enforced, or not; and how forum selection, evidence standards and political timing shape outcomes. Three situations anchor the discussion: Myanmar, Israel and Palestine, and Ukraine.
Compulsory
Reading
A Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression Against Ukraine: Origins, Debates, and Implications (Yale Journal of International Law, February 2026).On the choice of forum and its consequences. Open access.
Listening
Asymmetrical Haircuts, Justice Update: Genocide Evidence and Intent with Gambia and Myanmar.On the ICJ merits case. Open access.
Watching
Watch a public hearing of the International Court of Justice (Gambia v. Myanmar or South Africa v. Israel), on the ICJ site or UN Web TV.Primary source.
Optional
  • Myanmar: ICJ, The Gambia v. Myanmar (Case 178), case page and merits (hearings concluded 29 January 2026).Primary source.
  • Myanmar: Independent Investigative Mechanism for Myanmar (IIMM).UN mechanism.
  • Ukraine: Council of Europe, Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression against Ukraine.Primary source.
  • Israel and Palestine: ICJ, Application of the Genocide Convention in the Gaza Strip (South Africa v. Israel), filed 29 December 2023; provisional measures ordered, merits pending.Primary source.
  • South Africa, Department of International Relations and Cooperation, South Africa submits dossier to the ICJ on Israel's non-compliance with provisional measures orders.On the enforcement of provisional measures. Primary source.
  • DRC and Rwanda: Kerstin Bree Carlson, DRC has taken Rwanda to the world court over genocide again (The Conversation, July 2026).Accessible explainer.
  • Khan Khalid Adnan, From Fact-Finding to Fact-Proving at the ICJ: The Gambia v Myanmar (Opinio Juris, May 2026).On proving genocidal intent.
Discussion questions
Justice
  • For the Rohingya, for Palestinians and for Ukrainians, which forum offers what? What does the ICJ add that the ICC cannot, and the reverse?
  • How does the ICJ secure compliance with provisional measures? What remedies exist when a state ignores them?
Politics and power
  • Is third-state litigation under the Genocide Convention a principled defence of obligations owed to all, or the judicialisation of geopolitics? Can it be both?
  • Who can afford to litigate at the ICJ, and what does that asymmetry mean for the promise of equal justice?

Grading and Methodology

Assessment combines a written contribution on an emerging issue, oral advocacy through a moot statement, and active class participation.

Part I: Oral advocacy (30%)

Moot oral statement before the 2026 ICC Assembly of States Parties, delivered in Session 6, Tuesday 13 October 2026.
  • Each of you will represent a State (party or non-party to the Rome Statute), an intergovernmental organisation (IGO) or a non-governmental organisation (NGO) on a specific issue or challenge during the General Debate of the 2026 Assembly of States Parties. The Assembly's website carries the official documents and agenda: asp.icc-cpi.int
  • You may invent an NGO, as long as it is clearly identified
  • For inspiration, look at the General Debate statements from previous years
  • The statement is strictly limited to three minutes and is delivered in front of your fellow representatives. Three minutes is usually 300 to 500 words
  • You may read from a written statement or speak without notes. You do not need to send me the written version

Grading indicators

  • Presentation: argumentation and analysis, adapted to the audience
  • Structure: coherence and use of time
  • Performance: contact with the audience
  • Illustration: making the issue concrete and understandable

Deadline

  • Sunday 11 October 2026: email your choice of State, IGO or NGO, and the name you will act under (for example an ambassador before the United Nations)

Part II: Written contribution on an emerging ICL issue (50%)

  • A legal contribution resulting from your research on an emerging issue in international criminal law, to be submitted by Tuesday 8 December 2026, 23:59 Paris time, as a PDF by email to jeanne.sulzer@sciencespo.fr
  • The contribution should have a legal focus, with attention to current academic and advocacy debates, the state of the jurisprudence (if any), legal characterisation and procedural issues

Examples of issues currently debated in the field, with developments before domestic and international courts and involvement from civil society:

  • Immunities
  • International criminal law and the migration crisis
  • Corporate accountability for international crimes
  • Climate change, ecocide and international criminal law
  • Open source investigation (OSINT) and new technologies to document and prove international crimes
  • International criminal law and cultural heritage
  • Gender apartheid
  • State litigation at the ICJ and the enforcement of provisional measures

You may choose another topic, to be agreed in advance by email.

Format

  • Times New Roman 12, single spaced
  • Internal citations, with references listed alphabetically in MLA format
  • Length: 4,000 words, excluding references
  • No more than two text boxes; illustrations and pictures are welcome and do not count towards the word limit

What a strong contribution looks like

  • A clear, arguable thesis rather than a description
  • Evidence from primary sources (cases, statutes, official documents) and engagement with scholarly sources
  • Analysis rather than summary; every claim cited; a conclusion that returns to the thesis

Part III: Class participation and discussion (20%)

Class participation is central, and students are expected to contribute actively to the discussion. Be prepared to bring up a point, raise a question, connect ideas, offer your opinion, share your experience, agree or disagree with others. Listening carefully to others in class should prompt ideas of your own. Express them, and do so respectfully.

Use of artificial intelligence

This course follows Sciences Po's policy on artificial intelligence: AI as a lever for your skills, never a substitute for your own judgement, with documented and acknowledged use. I use these tools myself to prepare materials and slides, and I check every output against the primary sources.

You may use generative artificial intelligence for the written contribution on one condition: attach a short appendix reproducing the prompts you used and stating how you verified the output. The appendix does not count towards the word limit. Undeclared use is a breach of academic integrity; declared use earns transparency, not marks. The analysis remains your responsibility, and superficial work will be graded as such. For the moot, the statement you deliver must be your own.

No assessment will require a paid tool, and you must not submit personal, sensitive or confidential data to public artificial intelligence platforms.
